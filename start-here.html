Stephen's four rules of the front desk

  1. The goal people say first is rarely the goal. "I want to lose twenty pounds" is a number. "I could not carry my kid up the stairs on Sunday" is a reason. The second one survives February and the first one does not, so he asks until he gets to it.
  2. Nobody here works on commission, because nobody here sells anything. There is no assessment that turns into a package, no free session that becomes a contract. If a gym's first conversation with you is a price, that conversation was never about you.
  3. The licensed person wins. If Dr. Lena says stop, everyone stops, including the coach, including the manager, including the member who is annoyed about it. That rule is not negotiable and it is the reason the rest of the floor can be relaxed.
  4. Being handed to someone else is not being passed around. The most useful sentence at this desk is "that one is not mine, let me get you the right person." Stephen would rather send you three doors down than be the fourth confident wrong person you listened to.
Stephen checks the industry

Outnumbered, four to one

Stephen is the only male in his house and he will tell you it is the luckiest fact of his life. His wife, two preschool daughters, and the puppy: all girls, all louder than him, all completely in charge. The girls attend ETL You and are, in his words, deeply and unreasonably loyal to Everly Castle. The puppy has an official staff harness reading THE GYM DOG and no duties whatsoever.

It is not a footnote on the character. A man who spends his evenings taking orders from people under four feet tall has no ego left to defend at work, which is exactly why he can hand you to someone better without needing to be the one who fixed it.

He did not move to Dayton for this job. He moved because his wife's parents are getting older and needed family closer, and that got decided at a kitchen table in about twenty minutes. The job came after. He is a general manager who relocated for his in-laws, which tells you most of what you need to know before he opens his mouth.

And most Saturdays he is not here. He is with a group of teenage boys growing up without their fathers, and he bristles if you call it charity. The way he puts it: they need to watch a man show up on time, keep his word, be gentle with his wife and his daughters, and say I do not know out loud when he does not know. Not so they have a role model. So they can be one. The chain is the point, and he is clear that he is not the important link in it.

Saturday. He is not winning this and has no intention of trying.

Why the floor is built this way

At thirty-one, ten years out of the field, Stephen decided to get back in shape. He followed the loudest, most confident advice he could find and tore something in his shoulder that took a licensed physical therapist eleven months to unpick. He has a kinesiology degree. He had studied the exact tissue he tore. Nobody had lied to him, and knowing the material did not save him, because knowing the material is not the same as having somebody who knows you.

So the rules here are his: the licensed clinician can overrule anyone, including him. Every claim on the site names a source you can open. Nothing is sold. And the person at the front door is measured on whether he sent you to the right colleague, not on whether he kept you talking to him.
