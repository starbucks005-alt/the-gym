The thing nobody at a zero-proof bar tells you

Several things sold as non-alcoholic are not actually zero. In the United States a product can be labeled non-alcoholic and still contain up to 0.5 percent alcohol by volume. That is a rounding error if you are just cutting back. It is not a rounding error if you are in recovery, pregnant, managing liver disease, or taking a medication such as metronidazole or disulfiram that reacts with alcohol.

Wyatt tracks this on every drink, because he is on the same side of the bar as you:

  • Aromatic bitters are typically 35 to 45 percent alcohol. A dash is a very small absolute amount, but it is not none.
  • Kombucha ferments continuously and can drift above 0.5 percent, especially homebrewed or if it sat warm.
  • Non-alcoholic beer, wine, and spirits are usually under 0.5 percent, not zero.
  • Vanilla and most flavor extracts are alcohol-based.

Wyatt's four rules of the zero-proof glass

  1. Acid is what you are actually missing. Alcohol brings bite. Take it out and the drink goes flat and sweet, every time. Citrus, vinegar in small amounts, tart cherry, hibiscus, or a splash of brine put the edge back. If a mocktail tastes like juice, it needed acid, not more syrup.
  2. Bruise the herbs, do not shred them. One firm press with the back of a spoon releases the oils. Pulverizing releases chlorophyll, and chlorophyll tastes like a lawn. This is the single most common mistake.
  3. Carbonation goes in last, and you stir once. Every extra stir is bubbles you paid for and threw away. Build everything else, add the fizz, one gentle turn, done.
  4. Use the real glass. A coupe, a rocks glass, a decent highball. This sounds like theater and it is the most functional rule on the list, because the entire point is that you are holding a drink, not holding an apology.
