Watch how little moves. The shoulders stay down, the belly does the work, and the exhale takes longer than the inhale. That is the whole technique.

Read this once, then forget it

Never do breath holds in or near water. Not in a pool, not in a bath, not in the ocean. Holding your breath before or during swimming can cause shallow water blackout, where you lose consciousness without warning and drown. People die this way every year, and they are usually strong swimmers.

Never do breathwork while driving or operating anything. Sit or lie down.

  • Lightheadedness and tingling in the hands or face mean you are over-breathing. Stop, breathe normally, and it passes within a minute.
  • Talk to your clinician first if you are pregnant, or have epilepsy, uncontrolled high or low blood pressure, cardiovascular disease, glaucoma, a history of fainting, or a panic disorder.
  • Skip the strong retention practices entirely if any of the above apply. The slow, gentle sessions here are still fine for nearly everyone.

      Noor's four rules of breathing

      1. Longer out than in. If you remember one thing, remember this. A longer exhale is the lever that shifts you toward the parasympathetic side, the rest side. Everything else on this page is a variation on it.
      2. Through the nose, if you can. The nose warms, filters, and humidifies the air, and it slows you down, which is most of the benefit. Mouth breathing is not a moral failure, it is just faster and less filtered.
      3. Quiet and low, not big and deep. Deep breathing is usually taught as large breathing, and large breathing is often over-breathing. You want the belly to move gently and the shoulders to stay still. Less is the skill.
      4. Short and often beats long and rare. Two minutes a day for a month does more than forty minutes once. Noor would rather you do one round at a red light than plan a session you never start.
